Low-prefix transfer-guard obstruction

The low-prefix corridor does not force the right-edge fold-transfer guard. The admitted word (1,2,2,6,2) has low-prefix length 2, but its right-edge diagonal violates the transfer guard. The actual seeded PGS endpoint prefixes still satisfy the guard on the measured surface.

Right-edge fold guard

For an admitted prefix, the right-edge diagonal folds a candidate next gap into the next leading value. The backward ceiling starts at 2; every transfer lane must be at most the current ceiling to keep admitted even candidates as one initial interval.

Formal Obstruction

Lemma. Seeded prefix admission plus low-prefix length at least 2 does not imply the right-edge fold-transfer guard.

Take the seeded word W=(1,2,2,6,2). Its absolute-difference cascade is:

1 2 2 6 2
1 0 4 4
1 4 0
3 4
1

So W is admitted: the final leading value is 1. Its non-leading left prefix begins 2,2, so ell(W)=2. Its right-edge diagonal is 2,4,0,4,1. Removing the terminal admitted 1 and running the backward transfer ceiling gives a failure at lane value 4 against ceiling 2.

The extension candidates expose the damage. For positive even candidate next gaps up to 30, the admitted set is {2,4,8,10,12}. It is not the initial interval {2,4,6,...}, because 6 is rejected. A proof that relies on a single upper bound for the next endpoint gap therefore needs the actual PGS transfer guard, not only the low-prefix corridor.

Proof-Tree Change

The low-prefix depletion and reset corridor remains useful, but it cannot be the whole bridge to right-edge fold admission. The proof must add an actual ordered PGS endpoint-gap exclusion law for the guard-failure family beginning with (1,2,2,6,2), or prove another PGS invariant that supplies the same right-edge guard.

This shifts the next attack away from a generic low-prefix theorem and toward actual endpoint-gap grammar: why seeded PGS produces the 2,4 reset admission boundary instead of the abstract 2,6 guard-failure boundary.
