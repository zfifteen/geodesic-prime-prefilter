Boundary reserve exclusion

Actual PGS does contain high low-prefix boundary pairs such as 2,6. They occur with enough low-prefix reserve for ordinary depletion to keep the leading edge safe. The live theorem target is reserve separation from the floor, not absolute exclusion of the 2,6 boundary.

PGS Objects

Low-prefix reserve

For cascade row R, ell(R) is the number of consecutive non-leading left lanes in {0,2}. The floor is ell=2, because one ordinary depletion step then leaves only ell=1 unless a reset begins.

Boundary exit

The boundary pair is R_ell, R_{ell+1}. The prior abstract obstruction begins with floor reserve and boundary 2,6: (1,2,2,6,2).

Reserve-Safety Lemma

Lemma. If a cascade row has leading value 1, all non-leading lanes even, and ell(R) >= 3, then one ordinary difference step preserves leading value 1 and leaves ell(Delta R) >= 2, independently of the boundary pair.

Proof. The first non-leading lane is either 0 or 2, so |R_1 - R_0| = 1. Every adjacent pair inside the first ell non-leading lanes is drawn from {0,2}, so each corresponding difference is also in {0,2}. Therefore the next row has at least ell-1 low-prefix lanes. If ell >= 3, then ell(Delta R) >= 2.

The boundary pair matters only at the floor. A high boundary after a long low-prefix corridor is safe for one step by reserve alone.

Proof-Tree Change

The previous abstract obstruction suggested excluding the 2,6 boundary family. That route is false on actual PGS rows: 2,6 appears six times on the measured surface.

The usable PGS target is reserve separation. The theorem to prove is: high boundary pairs can occur only after the low-prefix corridor has enough reserve, while the floor boundary is forced to the reset-admission pair 2,4. This is the exact form needed by the Reserve-Safety Lemma.
