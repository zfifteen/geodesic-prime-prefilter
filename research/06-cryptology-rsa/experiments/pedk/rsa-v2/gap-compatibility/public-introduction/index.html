Finding Factors Without Factorizing: A Prime Gap Grammar Research Branch

The bold thesis is that the gap containing a composite number carries information about the gap neighborhoods around its prime factors. The current evidence does not prove a theorem and does not recover factors, but it shows a strong measured grammar of compatibility and exclusion.

A Project About Gaps Between Primes

This research project studies the exact structure of the spaces between consecutive prime numbers. A prime gap is the stretch of integers after one prime and before the next. Most familiar discussions treat such a gap mainly by its length, but the integers inside the gap also have arithmetic structure. The project asks whether that structure is rich enough to explain how primes are arranged, how composites sit between them, and how the local neighborhood of one number can constrain the possible neighborhoods of related numbers.

2

Gap Types And Grammar

A gap between primes is not just an empty interval. Each integer inside it has divisors, residue classes, distances from the endpoints, and a position relative to the other integers in the same interval. Two gaps with the same length can still differ because the arithmetic inside them is arranged differently. When those differences repeat, we can speak of gap types. When certain types tend to appear before, after, or around other types in structured ways, we can speak of a grammar.

Finding Factors Without Factorizing

A composite number made from two primes sits in a public location: anyone can find the consecutive primes that surround the composite number and inspect the gap that contains it. Its two prime factors are hidden, but each factor also sits at the endpoint of its own neighboring gaps. The research question is whether the public gap around the composite number restricts the possible gap neighborhoods around the two hidden factors. If some factor-neighborhood patterns are incompatible with the public gap pattern, then those possibilities can be excluded without testing divisibility by candidate factors.

What Did Not Work

The first attempts were too coarse. A raw gap label around the composite number was not enough to determine which factor neighborhoods were compatible. Position inside the gap helped, but position alone also collapsed different situations into the same bucket. Broad claims about uniform factor neighborhoods failed: some fully uniform neighborhoods do occur. A narrow all-o6 corner produced useful evidence, but several broad all-o6 exclusion rules were falsified as the tested bands moved upward.

Approach Status What it taught us
Raw public gap type too coarse The same broad public label can admit different factor-neighborhood behavior.
Position inside the gap alone too coarse Position matters, but it must be combined with the gap's internal type.
Uniform-pattern exclusion falsified Fully uniform factor neighborhoods can occur, so uniformity itself is not forbidden.
Broad all-o6 exclusion falsified The all-o6 corner is informative, but only under a more detailed public word.

The Methods That First Produced Signal

The first real signal appeared when the experiment stopped asking whether one public label excluded one factor label everywhere, and instead used held-out tests. A rule was formed on one range of known examples and then checked on a later range. This removed many finite-range coincidences. Adding a coarse phase for where the composite number sits inside its gap sharpened the signal. After that, residue patterns in the four factor-side gaps began to stand out, especially patterns involving repeated o6 residues.

At this point the project vocabulary becomes useful. The public object is now a word describing the gap around the composite number N. The factor object is an unordered word describing the two neighborhoods around the hidden primes p and q. The working map is written as S(N) -> F(p,q): public structure around the composite number points to compatible or incompatible factor-neighborhood structures.

First signal: held-out and forward tests turned raw absence into a survivor surface. Many candidate exclusions failed, but some survived long enough to show that the map was not random noise.
6

The Methods That Produced Strong Signal

The strong signal appeared when the public side was treated as a grammar word instead of a single label. The word includes the reduced type of the previous gap, the exact subtype of the gap containing N, the phase of N inside that gap, a position relative to the selected point inside the gap, and the reduced type of the following gap. In project terms, that selected point is the local GWR point, and the useful public coordinate is the GWR-relative distance.

This changed the problem from looking for one magic feature to building a multiplication map. The map compares public words around N against unordered factor-neighborhood words around p and q. Some cells in the map are observed. Other cells are absent even though both the public word and the factor word are well supported elsewhere. Those absent cells are the candidate incompatibilities.

The pipeline now has one more natural step. After a candidate incompatibility survives fresh slices, it should be applied back to the possible endpoint pairs (p, q). That turns a grammar rule into a measured endpoint-space reduction: how many possible endpoint pairs remain after public gap grammar removes incompatible neighborhoods.

Current Findings And Where We Stand

The current result is a strong measured compatibility signal. Across the current multiplication map, there are 23 supported public grammar words and 198 supported factor words. Their map contains 3741 absent cells. Compressing those absent cells by factor residue and winner-phase class gives 531 candidate class exclusions. A fresh targeted slice test then took the top five compressed candidates into later factor bands. Two survived and three were falsified.

The strongest lesson is that the factor-neighborhood law is conditional. A factor class with phase pattern mid:3|late:1 is not excluded everywhere. It is excluded only under specific public grammar words. Neighbor context matters. The residue mix matters. The exact containing gap type matters. The research has moved from “there is a signal” to “the signal is a grammar of compatibility and incompatibility.”

Next milestone: endpoint-space reduction

The current exclusions act on factor-neighborhood classes. The next measurement converts those class exclusions into concrete endpoint-space reduction. For a fixed composite number, first read the public word around the gap that contains it. Then list the possible endpoint pairs in the selected interval, describe the neighboring prime gaps around each endpoint pair, and remove the pairs whose combined neighborhood words are incompatible with the public word.

This is the bridge from a grammar signal to a search-space number. The output is no longer just “this factor-neighborhood class was absent.” The output becomes “this many possible endpoint pairs (p, q) were eliminated by public gap grammar before candidate divisibility was tested.”

Step Measured object Reduction question
1 Public word around the composite number Which factor-neighborhood words are incompatible with this public word?
2 Endpoint pairs in the chosen factor interval Which endpoint pairs carry one of the incompatible neighborhood words?
3 Remaining endpoint-pair set What fraction of possible endpoint pairs (p, q) survived?

A significant benchmark is reached when this measured endpoint-pair reduction exceeds the reduction supplied by an existing classical method at comparable cost. The present documents do not claim that milestone. They define the compatibility surface needed to measure it.

Current boundary: this is strong measured evidence for a gap-grammar compatibility law. It is not a theorem, not a completed factor recovery method, and not live factor recovery. The next step is to keep compressing the surviving rules, test them on fresh targeted public slices, and convert surviving class exclusions into measured (p, q) endpoint-pair reduction.
